Chaos, dissipation and Holland's seven elements

To predict is to follow step by step. Part two: from deterministic chaos to dissipative self-organisation and the seven elements with which a living system learns to adapt.

3. Deterministic chaos: to predict is to follow step by step

Chaos theory is, in Vera Ortiz's account, the intellectual heir of the complexity sciences, first formulated in the article "Deterministic Non Periodic Flow", written by Edward Lorenz, meteorologist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, in 1963. His intuitions would give rise to two scientific revolutions of immeasurable impact: chaos theory and the complexity sciences. James Gleick documented it in Chaos. Making a New Science (1988), and the Santa Fe collective — Doyne Farmer, Norman Packard, James Crutchfield and Robert Shaw, the Dynamical Systems Collective, colloquially known as the Chaos Cabal — developed its study, seeking mathematical ways to characterise the properties of the strange attractors that Lorenz had discovered.

The operative definition of chaos that Vera Ortiz takes up is James Trefil's, and it deserves to be quoted because it is the exact limit of every predictive claim:

«resultado final depende muy sensiblemente de las condiciones iniciales... Para todas las finalidades prácticas, el comportamiento de los sistemas caóticos no puede predecirse... Los sistemas caóticos son no lineales» (Trefil, 1001 cosas que todo el mundo debería saber sobre ciencia, pp. 263-264, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 39).

Chaos theory is a mathematical theory whose object of analysis is systems that display unpredictable behaviour which, at first sight, looks apparently random, although in reality their components are governed by strictly deterministic laws. Until its emergence, physics in general — and quantum mechanics in particular — had dealt with systems that were completely predictable in principle, at least at large scale; yet the natural world shows an intrinsic and inalienable tendency towards chaotic behaviour. Meteorology is a rich source of examples; others are the turbulence in rising columns of smoke, the beating of the human heart, variations in the climate, the behaviour of natural networks, the functioning of physiological systems, the evolution of the cosmos, of nature and of species, the immune response to an exogenous agent, and the organisation of the global economy. Since nothing can be measured with absolute precision, chaos gives rise to a practical impossibility that is also a moral lesson: small differences in the input quickly become totalising differences in the output.

For some physicists, the science of chaos addresses processes and not states; "it is the study of becoming rather than of being" (Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 42). The science of chaos has broken the formerly impassable disciplinary borders, since it defines itself as the general science of nature, of systems, of the universal behaviour of complexity. Criticising reductionism, it presents itself as the science of the whole.

That lesson has a corollary that Paul Halpern formulates with a precision Vera Ortiz preserves, and that is the scientific justification of ℛenova's golden rule — verify instead of believing:

«Los sistemas caóticos son computacionalmente irreducibles. El estado del sistema, en un tiempo dado, puede sólo ser "anticipado al seguir la dinámica paso a paso"» (Halpern, The Pursuit of Destiny, p. 139, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 40).

A chaotic system cannot be predicted without following it step by step. There is no analytical shortcut, no closed formula, no oracle. A market, an audience, a community of readers, a store with 140 records and three payment links are systems of this kind: their future behaviour is not deduced from their catalogue, it is followed in their dynamics, day by day, sale by sale, checkout session by checkout session. That is why ℛenova Press publishes no sales forecasts and promises no income: it records, reconciles and verifies. Computational irreducibility is not an excuse for not knowing; it is the reason why knowing demands tracking.

4. Dissipative structures and self-organisation: becoming before being

If chaos is the limit of prediction, dissipative structures are its creative counterpart: order born far from equilibrium. Ilya Prigogine, Nobel laureate in Chemistry, built the thermodynamics of systems far from equilibrium, and Vera Ortiz devotes the core of his second section of conceptual history to him. Prigogine notes ironically that, much to his regret, "Einstein has turned out to be, against his will, the Darwin of physics" (Prigogine, El nacimiento del tiempo, p. 16, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 8): Einstein's universe is a universe in evolution, not an eternal mechanism.

The starting point of Prigogine's reflection is instability, and its formulation is a complete programme in two sentences:

«La inestabilidad, el caos, nos obligan a pasar a un esquema probabilista (abandono de las trayectorias en mecánica clásica, de las funciones de onda en mecánica cuántica), el esquema probabilista nos lleva a estudiar el operador de evolución correspondiente, lo cual nos permitirá aclarar la ruptura de simetría temporal y por consiguiente la irreversibilidad... La inestabilidad nos obliga a adoptar de nuevo una descripción estadística» (Prigogine, Las leyes del caos, pp. 34 y 53-55, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, pp. 14-15).

In open systems, far from equilibrium, the dissipation of energy generates structures that maintain and adapt themselves. Non-linearity in a dissipative structure is the property by which the structure acts on itself and triggers its own dissipation of energy. Self-organisation needs no master planner: it needs energy, openness and non-linearity. Alan Turing had demonstrated this earlier, in the field of morphogenesis, and Vera Ortiz takes it up through Steven Johnson: Turing "demonstrated using mathematical tools how an organism could assemble itself without any master planner" (Johnson, Emergence, p. 14, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 123). Turing's scheme had shown how, from simple mathematical rules, complex structures could arise. This marked the beginning of the sciences of self-organisation, which would later give rise to the complexity sciences.

Richard N. Adams and Leslie White carried that physics into anthropology and social theory: social evolution as the self-organisation of energy. White sought, anticipating Adams by four decades, to provide a "dynamic interpretation of cultural development in terms of its most fundamental factor: energy" (White, The Science of Culture, p. 362, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 155). Adams begins his model with evidence that strikes him as basic and that is, at the same time, a warning for any publishing project that believes itself capable of planning its own success:

«La complejidad producida por las cosas mismas ha probado ser el proceso invisible más difícil de comprender» (Adams, The Eight Day, p. xiii, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 155).

The copy of Vera Ortiz's thesis ends abruptly at this point, on page 159, mid-sentence, in the analysis of Adams's dissipative structures. That cut is not a bibliographical detail: it is a lesson about the state of knowledge. Every work, including this one, ends mid-sentence; the honest thing is to mark where the text breaks off and not to invent its continuation. ℛenova Press applies the same rule to its catalogue: if a book is in draft, it says it is in draft; if a delivery is coordinated and not automatic, it says it is coordinated and not automatic; if a figure was not verified, it is marked as unverified. Self-organisation does not exempt one from verification: it demands it, because a system that organises itself is also a system that can deceive itself.

The connection with ℛenova is direct, and it is physical before it is metaphorical. ℛenova says A → A′: everything that exists contains the possibility of becoming something new. In Prigogine's language, that is becoming before being: a system far from equilibrium transforms itself and generates new order without a master planner. A publishing house in permanent crisis — 140 records, three products, a store on the Advanced plan charging $399 USD per month, a newly installed blog — is a dissipative system: it consumes energy (work, attention, money) and, if the dissipation is well governed, produces structure (catalogue, community, income). If it is badly governed, dissipation produces only entropy: records without purchase, products without a master, campaigns never executed. The difference between one thing and the other is not inspiration: it is procedure.

5. Complex adaptive systems: Holland's seven elements

The operative model of all the above is the complex adaptive system (CAS), and its central theorist is John Holland, professor of psychology and electrical engineering at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, author of Hidden Order. How Adaptation Builds Complexity (1995) and Emergence. From Chaos to Order (1999). Vera Ortiz presents him as the guru of emergence, and his central aphorism is also the most cited in his work:

«la conducta de todo un sistema adaptativo complejo es más que la suma de las conductas de sus partes» (Holland, Hidden Order, p. 5, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 105).

CAS share non-linearity with chaotic systems, but they possess something the latter lack: adaptability. Adaptation is the sine qua non of CAS. Holland defines it as the process by which an organism fits itself to its environment. Many CAS have a peculiar property that distinguishes them from simple chaotic systems: a tiny input produces enormous predictable and directed changes — that is, an amplification effect. In CAS, non-linearities abound, and this implies that we cannot generalise or use the common methods of quantitative analysis; we will then have to employ comparative, essentially qualitative methods.

The seven basic elements of CAS are four properties and three mechanisms common to all of them (Holland, Hidden Order, pp. 10-40, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, pp. 105-107). They are worth setting out with the detail Vera Ortiz devotes to them, because they are the operations manual of this essay and of the publishing house that is its case:

A) The property of aggregation. In a first sense, a standard way of simplifying complex systems: we aggregate similar things into categories and then treat them as equivalent; it is a technique for building models. In a second sense, it concerns what the CAS does and not how we model it: it concerns the emergence of complex large-scale behaviour from the aggregated interactions of less complex agents. Aggregates formed in this way can act at a higher level as meta-agents; in this second sense, aggregation is a basic characteristic of every CAS, and the emergent phenomena that result constitute its most enigmatic aspect.

B) The tagging mechanism. It facilitates the formation of aggregates; it is a prototypical mechanism for aggregation and the formation of boundaries in CAS. Systems use tags to manipulate symmetries. Interactions based on well-established identifiers provide the basis for filters, specialisation and cooperation. This leads to the emergence of meta-agents and persistent organisations, even when their elements change constantly.

C) The property of non-linearity. Linearity implies the possibility of obtaining the value of a whole by adding the values of its parts. Even in the simplest situations, non-linearities can interfere with a linear approach to aggregates. Non-linear interactions almost always make the behaviour of the aggregate more complicated than could be foreseen from the sum of its components or their average.

D) The property of flow. Nodes are processors — agents — and connectors designate the possible interactions. In CAS, the flow in these networks varies over time; indeed, nodes and connections can appear or disappear depending on whether the agents adapt or fail in the attempt. Thus neither the flow nor the networks remain fixed. There are two properties of flows: the multiplier effect, when additional resources are injected into a node and pass from node to node producing a chain of changes; and the recycling effect, the effect of cycles in the network.

E) The property of diversity. Diversity is neither accidental nor random. On the contrary, the persistence of any individual agent depends on the context provided by other agents. Each type of agent occupies a niche defined by the interactions centred on that agent.

F) The mechanism of internal modelling. Another typical hallmark of CAS is their capacity for anticipation. The basic move in building models is the elimination of detail so that the selected patterns are emphasised. Since models are internal to the agent, the agent must select patterns in the stream of stimuli it receives and must convert those patterns into changes in its internal structure. Finally, the changes in the structure — the model — must enable the agent to anticipate the subsequent consequences when the pattern — or something similar — is encountered again.

G) The building-block mechanism. In real situations, the internal model must be based on limited samples of a perpetually changing environment. Building blocks serve to find regularities in a complex world. This use of blocks to generate internal models is a characteristic feature of complex adaptive systems.

Reading Holland in editorial terms is immediate. Aggregation is the catalogue: 140 records grouped into collections, series and bundles, which act as meta-agents — a series, a collection, an edition — when treated as a whole. Tagging is the brand: the Portal of three arches, the motto "Living thought in the form of a work", price as a tag ($100, $219, $314) that filters, specialises and enables cooperation between products. Non-linearity is the sale: a single post, a single podcast, a single video can produce a disproportionate amplification effect, and no linear model of "so many posts, so many sales" captures it. Flow is the circulation of money, files and attention: nodes and connections that appear and disappear as agents adapt or fail. Diversity is the catalogue itself: each work occupies a niche defined by its interactions, and the persistence of a work depends on the context the others give it. Internal modelling is CENTRO: evaluating before acting is building an internal model of risk and anticipating its consequences. Building blocks are the method of this essay and of the imprint: finding regularities in a complex world from limited samples — twelve charges, three checkout sessions, a five-day window without sales — without pretending that the sample is the universe.
