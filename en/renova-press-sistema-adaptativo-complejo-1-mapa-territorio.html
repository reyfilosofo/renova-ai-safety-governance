Introduction

Some ideas stay in the head. And some ideas demand form. ℛenova Press was born of that second kind of idea: it is the publishing imprint of Rey Filósofo and SERESARTE, and its work is turning ℛenova thought into books, essays, dossiers and works that can be read, touched, shared and read again. Its motto, written in its own storekit, sums it up in one line: living thought in the form of a work. This essay is not an advertising manifesto for that imprint. It is an academic essay that asks whether a publishing house can be thought with the most demanding categories of contemporary science, and whether that thinking changes anything in the way it is governed.

The guide for that journey is someone else's work, and it is worth saying so from the first page: Iván Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas. El pensamiento complejo, los nuevos paradigmas de la ciencia y las ciencias de la complejidad (2016), a thesis of 159 pages and 65,381 words, whose copy used here ends abruptly on page 159, mid-sentence, in the analysis of the dissipative structures of Richard N. Adams, without conclusions or a final bibliography in that file. The conclusions do exist, however, in another document by the same author — "La sensibilidad posmoderna y el pensamiento de la crisis", presented by Sergio Iván Vera Ortiz — and they are the ones cited in the final section of this essay. The file that circulated under the name "TESIS_FINAL_YVES" is not the work of any Yves: it is the same text of Vera Ortiz's conclusions, and the file name is an error that is corrected and recorded here. Vera Ortiz is a third party; his work does not belong to the ℛenova corpus and it is always cited with attribution to him.

The thesis defended here is modest, and for that reason it is strong: a publishing house is a complex adaptive system, and governing it demands the method of complexity: verify before believing, model before acting, publish in batches and declare the limits. The essay proceeds in seven movements. First, the crisis of classical rationality as the immediate background of everything else. Second, the two cultures and the third culture as a cultural diagnosis. Third, deterministic chaos and computational irreducibility as a limit of prediction. Fourth, dissipative structures and self-organisation as the physics of becoming. Fifth, Holland's complex adaptive systems as the operative model. Sixth, emergence, its power and its warning. Seventh, the documentary case: ℛenova Press as of 24 November 2026, with its figures, its discrepancies and its pending decisions. The conclusions are formulated, in the manner of Vera Ortiz, as a series of theses.

1. The crisis of rationality: the map is not the territory

Vera Ortiz opens his panorama with a statement that is at once a historical diagnosis and a programmatic thesis:

«Las ciencias de la complejidad simbolizan la más reciente y vasta avanzada teórica de Occidente, así como representan el testamento teórico del ideal neopositivista de la ciencia unificada. Coinciden temática y temporalmente con la posmodernidad y el reflujo del relativismo exacerbado propio de la situación cultural que atraviesan las sociedades que habitan el capitalismo tardío; comparten un mismo clima intelectual: la sensibilidad posmoderna. Las ciencias de la complejidad (la ciencia de la totalidad, o el pensamiento complejo) es uno de los múltiples modos de acceder a lo real. Se presenta el pensamiento complejo a sí mismo como la perspectiva holística del universo, la naturaleza, el hombre, el pensamiento, el lenguaje, etc.» (Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 1).

The immediate background of that climate is the collapse of unitary and strong discourses, and its sharpest formula is also the most cited in the whole thesis:

«La racionalidad clásica confundió el instrumento del conocimiento con la realidad, el mapa con el territorio.» (Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 1).

Classical rationality, Vera Ortiz explains, presented itself as a natural, necessary and a priori structure, responsible for all cognitive and logical possibility; it operated a regime of abstract language that believed it founded a central, exclusive and invariable order in which the play of all the possibilities of the various phenomena was precodified. Superorder and harmony were rooted in reality. That claim collapses from the mid-nineteenth century onwards, with Maxwell's electromagnetism, which stands as a refutation of Newton's physical theory; with relativity, which regards space and time as mere logical-geometrical figures; with quantum mechanics, which introduces indeterminism and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle; and with the crisis of the foundations of mathematics, with its non-Euclidean geometries and Cantor's paradoxes of the infinite.

Aldo Gargani, editor of Crisis de la razón (1983), specifies the scope of that collapse in a long quotation that Vera Ortiz reproduces and that is worth reproducing in full, because it is the death certificate of the classical order and, at the same time, its birth certificate as a problem:

«La crisis de la racionalidad clásica es también la crisis de una racionalidad moral y social formalmente análoga... La crisis del sistema clásico de la razón, el descubrimiento de lagunas y vacíos, han estado signados por la aparición de nuevas necesidades y por consiguiente de nuevos modos de ver alternativas allí donde nos habían enseñado que existía una posibilidad sola... No se trató de la catástrofe o del colapso de una forma de racionalidad por autoagotamiento. El viejo orden entra en crisis porque es destronado por uno nuevo» (Gargani, en Gargani [ed.], Crisis de la razón, pp. 44-45, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 2).

Vera Ortiz does not accept that formula without refining it. Dominique Lecourt warns that relativity did not appear to physicists as the announcement of a crisis but, on the contrary, as the way out of the unease that had been coming since the 1850s; far from being the announcement of the crisis, it was regarded "as the revolutionary way out of a 'crisis' and not as a sign of it" (Lecourt, El orden y los juegos, p. 73, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 2). The methodological lesson is permanent and it is the first rule of ℛenova: a crisis is not decreed, it is documented; and what looks like a collapse may be a way out that does not yet have a name.

Quantum mechanics carried that lesson to its extreme. Werner Heisenberg expresses it crudely, in words that Vera Ortiz takes from La imagen de la naturaleza en la física actual (p. 14):

«Cuando queremos formarnos una imagen del modo de ser de las partículas elementales, nos hallamos ante la fundamental imposibilidad de hacer abstracción de los procesos físicos mediante los cuales ganamos acceso a la observación de aquellas partículas. [...] Resulta de ello, en definitiva, que las leyes naturales que se formulan matemáticamente en la teoría cuántica no se refieren ya a las partículas elementales en sí, sino a nuestro conocimiento de dichas partículas... La noción de la realidad objetiva de las partículas elementales se ha disuelto» (Heisenberg, La imagen de la naturaleza en la física actual, p. 14, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 10).

The observer ceases to be an outside witness and becomes part of what is observed. That is the root of the second-order epistemology that Edgar Morin would later build, with the absolute centrality of the knowing subject, and it is also the root of ℛenova's discipline of evidence: if the instrument takes part in what is observed, then the instrument must be declared, calibrated and verified. A purchase link does not prove a payment; a published record does not prove a delivery; a simulation does not prove external validation. The confusion between the map and the territory is not an abstract philosophical error: it is the operational error a publishing house commits when it sells what it does not yet have.

2. The two cultures and the third culture: the dialogue of the deaf

The emergence of the complexity sciences coincides, in Vera Ortiz's panorama, with the third culture. The starting point is C. P. Snow, scientist and novelist, who in 1959 published Las dos culturas, a text that set out two opposed variants of the culture of his time: one, proper to literary people, confined the humanities to an enclosure incompatible with any scientific knowledge; the other, the scientific one, belonged to those with a solid scientific education who lacked humanistic culture and despised reading. Snow says it with a harshness that Vera Ortiz preserves intact:

«Aún se complacen en sostener que la cultura tradicional es toda la "cultura", como si el orden natural no existiera. Como si la exploración del orden natural no fuese del menor interés ni en su propia valía ni por sus consecuencias» (Snow, Las dos culturas y un segundo enfoque, pp. 23-24, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 3).

Between the two poles there was an abyss of mutual incomprehension, because the professional image each group had of the other was distorted. And yet it was possible to conjecture that the collision between the two cultures might bring enormous creative possibilities. In 1963, Snow published in the second edition of his work an article added at the last minute, "Las dos culturas: una segunda mirada", in which he self-critically admits two errors: not having emphasised enough the lack of communication between the two cultures, and not having managed to see the small, diffuse interstices in which a new kind of communication was being generated. His self-criticism is exemplary in its honesty:

«En mi exposición sobre la falta de comunicación entre las dos culturas, no exageraba; en todo caso me quedaba corto» (Snow, Las dos culturas y un segundo enfoque, p. 82, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 3).

And his prophecy, with its typo preserved:

«Anduve remiso en observar el desenvolvimiento de lo que [...] está convirtiéndose en algo así como una tercera cultura. Es acaso demasiado protnto [sic] para hablar de una tercera cultura existente. Pero ahora estoy convencido de que esta prelude el surgimiento probable de una tercera cultura en la que hubiera una comunicación más sólida, auténtica y plena, un diálogo constructivo y crítico entre los cultores de las antiguas dos culturas.» (Snow, Las dos culturas y un segundo enfoque, p. 81, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, pp. 3-4).

Four decades later, John Brockman assesses that third culture, the one that unites scientists concerned with the most pressing human problems, and argues that Snow's ideal has not been realised: contrary to his prediction, scientists and humanists still do not understand each other and still hold dialogues of the deaf. But there is a variation: scientists are communicating with the general public. And Brockman defines the strength of that movement in a quotation that is, at once, an editorial programme:

«la fuerza de la tercera cultura estriba en que admite desacuerdos acerca de las ideas que merecen tomarse en serio... Los pensadores de la tercera cultura son los nuevos intelectuales públicos.... A través de la historia, la vida intelectual ha estado marcada por el hecho de que sólo un número reducido de personas se ha dedicado a reflexionar por todos los demás. Lo que estamos presenciando es el paso de la antorcha de un grupo de intelectuales, los intelectuales de letras tradicionales, a un nuevo grupo, los intelectuales de la tercera cultura que emerge» (Brockman, «Introducción. La tercera cultura que emerge», en Brockman [ed.], La tercera cultura, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 4).

ℛenova Press stands exactly in that interstice that Snow did not manage to see and that Brockman describes: a publishing imprint that publishes philosophy with the rigour of science and science in the form of culture; a catalogue that runs from the treatise on biosemiotics to the personalised edition of Qualia, from the seven-page book-object to the podcast and the video. The third culture is not a slogan for Press: it is its business model and its criterion for publication. If a text cannot hold up before a scientist and before a reader of poetry at the same time, it is not yet ready. And the dialogue of the deaf that Snow and Brockman denounce is also the internal risk of every publishing house: that the catalogue should speak and nobody listen, that the store should have 140 records and no conversation.
