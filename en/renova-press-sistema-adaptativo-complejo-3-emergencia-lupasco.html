Emergence and its warning: what the whole knows that the parts do not know

The whole is more than the sum of the parts — but nothing can be concluded from the notion of complexity in itself. Part three: emergence, the pattern that connects, and Lupasco's warning.

Translator's note: long quotations are preserved in the original Spanish.

Scope note. Essay with the formal rigour of an international standard ("Scopus level"). It is not a publication in Scopus, it is not indexed in Scopus and it has not undergone peer review.

6. Emergence, the pattern that connects, and Lupasco's warning

Emergence is, in Roger Lewin's formulation, which Vera Ortiz makes his own, "the central characteristic of complex systems" (Lewin, Complexity, p. xi, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 123). Steven Johnson defines it with a formula that has become canonical:

«El movimiento de reglas de bajo nivel a una sofisticación de mayor nivel en lo que llamamos emergencia» (Johnson, Emergence, p. 18, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 124).

Johnson adds that the concepts of emergence and complexity are intimately linked with concepts as traditional in philosophical culture as chaos and order: "that mixture of order and anarchy is what we now call emergent behaviour" (Johnson, Emergence, p. 38, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 126). And he illustrates it with the city, which is also the best image of a publishing house: "A city is a kind of pattern-amplification machine: its neighbourhoods are a way of measuring and expressing the repeated behaviour of larger collectivities — capturing information about group behaviour and sharing that information with the group" (Johnson, Emergence, p. 40, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 126). A publishing house is a city of that kind: its collections are neighbourhoods that capture information about readers' behaviour and share it with the group. And as in the city, the largest patterns can emerge from uncoordinated local actions, and diverse forms and structures emerge from apparent chaos.

Emergence has a domain par excellence, and it is life. Ricard Solé and Brian Goodwin state it in a sentence that Vera Ortiz takes up as the epigraph of a whole science of emergent forms: "Life is... the domain of emergence par excellence..." (Solé y Goodwin, Signs of Life, p. x, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 88). Goodwin emphatically affirms that "natural selection is not the cause of organisms. Genes do not cause organisms. There are no causes of organisms. Organisms are self-caused agents" (cit. en Lewin, Complexity, p. 180, apud Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 88). Life self-organises; a publishing house, if it is a living system, does too.

But emergence has its warning, and Vera Ortiz puts it in the mouth of Stéphane Lupasco, in a quotation that should preside over any serious use of the word "emergence", including this essay:

«nada puede concluirse de la noción de complejidad en sí misma. Se pretende a veces, en nuestros días, por medio de una mágica idea de emergencia, hacer surgir propiedades características de la noción de complejidad, especialmente las peculiares de la vida» (Lupasco, Las tres materias, p. 17, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 123).

The warning is also an editorial rule: no property — a sale, a community, a validation — can be made to appear, as if by magic, from the mere invocation of complexity. Emergence is not a spell; it is a result that is verified or not verified. That is why this essay always distinguishes between what is verified, what is reported and what is hypothetical, and why the section that follows is documentary before it is rhetorical.

The search for the pattern that connects is the other name of that discipline. Gregory Bateson asks, in Espíritu y naturaleza (p. 18):

«¿Por qué los establecimientos educativos no enseñan casi nada acerca de la pauta que conecta? ... ¿Cuál es la pauta que conecta a todas las criaturas vivientes?» (Bateson, Espíritu y naturaleza, p. 18, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 68).

Heinz von Foerster finds that "systemics is the art of seeing, finding out and especially recognising connections between the entities observed" (von Foerster, Sistémica elemental, p. 10, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 68). All complexity is holistic, and in Bateson this leads to a spiritual and religious background. In sharp opposition, Niklas Luhmann builds a sociology without subjects. Between the two poles stands Morin's thought, which builds an "anti-method", a second-order epistemology from relational or network thought, with the absolute centrality of the knowing subject. His aim is to generate a propaedeutic of and towards complexity; from Vera Ortiz's perspective, that current "amounts to being a mere hermeneutic appreciation". The criticism is fair and it is also a self-criticism for this essay: complexity as a worldview is not enough; complexity as a method is needed, and the method is tested on a case.

Morin also offers one of the most fertile images in the whole thesis, that of the living machine and noise:

«la cibernética, en vez de ser una reducción simplista de esquemas mecánicos, constituye, al contrario, una introducción a la complejidad» (Morin, El paradigma perdido, p. 10, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 32).

And:

«mientras que el desorden interno, o en términos de teoría de la información, el "ruido" o el error, degrada constantemente a la máquina artificial, la máquina viva funciona siempre con una cierta proporción de "ruido", y el acrecentamiento de su complejidad, lejos de disminuir su tolerancia respecto al "ruido", la aumenta... Entre ciertos umbrales de tolerancia parece existir una íntima relación generativa entre el aumento de "ruido" o desorden y el de complejidad» (Morin, El paradigma perdido, pp. 27-28, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 32).

A living publishing house works with noise: with records in draft, with discrepancies between its sources, with a sales-free window, with a charge being retried. Noise is not the enemy; it is the material with which the living machine increases its complexity, provided it stays within certain tolerance thresholds. Editorial work is, in that sense, the art of keeping noise within the threshold where it generates order and outside the threshold where it generates collapse.

Complexity and mysticism deserve a paragraph of their own, because it is here that Vera Ortiz's thesis touches wisdom. Fritjof Capra, in El Tao de la física (1975), began the current that regards the contacts between the worldviews of science and mysticism as fundamental, and called for the founding of a new way of approaching reality:

«Los cambios originados por la física moderna..... parecen llevar hacia una misma dirección: hacia una visión que resulta muy parecida a la que presenta el misticismo oriental. Los conceptos de la física moderna muestran con frecuencia sorprendentes paralelismos con las filosofías religiosas del lejano Oriente» (Capra, El Tao de la física, p. 24, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 146).

Ken Wilber replied that Capra had confused different orders of being, and proposed six irreducible levels: matter, the biological sphere, psychology and philosophy, the subtle, the causal and ultimate consciousness. The thesis distinguishes four positions on the link between science and mysticism — radical negation, the weak nexus of worldviews and two more that the copy does not manage to develop, because the text breaks off before them — and that incompleteness is declared here exactly as it is. Wisdom, in this essay, is not an oriental ornament: it is the awareness that the map is not the territory, that life is the domain of emergence par excellence, and that the pattern that connects all living creatures is also the pattern that connects a reader with a book.

Danilo Zolo, lastly, gives the soberest definition of all, and the most useful for an editor: in his model, "complexity" does not describe objective properties of natural or social phenomena, nor does it denote complex objects as opposed to simple objects; it designates a cognitive situation in which individual or collective agents are forcibly immersed, one that becomes apparent when agents build and project relations in order to orient themselves in their environment, whether by predicting, arranging, planning, executing or manipulating (Zolo, Democracia y complejidad, 1994, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 151). And Zolo estimates that "in certain disciplines a series of rigorous definitions has been reached: for example, in dynamic topology, information theory, artificial intelligence research and, above all, in computer science, where the notion of computational complexity has been well established. Such formalised definitions are useful both in these and in other contexts, for calculation [...]" (Zolo, Democracia y complejidad, p. 16, cit. en Vera Ortiz, Las ciencias posmodernas, p. 129). Complexity, in short, is not a property of things: it is the situation of someone who must decide without knowing everything. That is exactly the situation of an editor.
